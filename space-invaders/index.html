Teaching an AI Space Invaders

An agent learned the 1980 Atari game from pixels and a score, the same way a person might: try something, see what happens, do more of what worked. Nobody wrote “shoot the invaders.” The method is PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization — a standard way to train a neural net by playing. This page is the 100 million step wrap-up.

If you only read one paragraph. A small convolutional network looks at four stacked screenshots, picks one of six joystick moves, and gets a tiny reward when the score goes up. After a batch of play, PPO updates the network a little — never a wild leap — then it plays again. Eight copies trained in parallel on a GPU; one window on stream played the current “best guess.” The run is 100 million environment steps on an RTX 4080 Super, now frozen so the same weights can play full games.

The loop

Every moment in the game is the same four beats. Repeat that tens of millions of times and the weights in the network slowly become a player.

See4 gray 84×84 frames
Choose6 buttons
PlayAtari 2600 ROM
Score+1 / 0 / −1
PPOnudge the net

The dashed idea: after PPO writes new weights, the next frames are seen with a slightly different player. Nothing is scripted.

What it actually sees and does

Eyes

Not objects, not “there is a ship at x,y.” The Arcade Learning Environment feeds the raw TV picture. We shrink it to 84×84 grayscale and stack the last four frames so motion is visible — shots, marching invaders, the cannon sliding.

Hands

Six discrete actions, the 2600 joystick plus fire: do nothing, fire, left, right, left+fire, right+fire. One shot on screen at a time, same as the original game.

Score signal

The network never sees “you have 2,400 points.” It only sees whether the last frame’s score went up, stayed, or (rarely) went down: +1, 0, or −1. That is a common Atari trick so a lucky 200-point saucer does not dominate learning.

Brain

A Nature CNN: three small convolution layers, then a 512-unit layer, then two heads. One head is the policy (how likely each button is). The other is the critic (a guess of how well the current screen will turn out). Same family of net used in the original DQN Atari work, trained here with PPO instead of Q-learning.

PPO, without the paper

PPO is an actor-critic method from OpenAI (Schulman et al., 2017). “Actor” is the player. “Critic” is a coach that scores the situation. You do not need the math to follow the idea:

  1. Play a short stretch. Eight games run at once, 128 steps each. That is about a thousand tiny decisions per update — a batch, not a whole lifetime.
  2. Ask “was that better than expected?” If the critic thought the screen was hopeless and then the score jumped, that action looks extra good (high advantage). If it already expected a hit, a hit is just average.
  3. Turn the good moves up a little. The policy is a pile of numbers. PPO increases the probability of actions that had positive advantage, decreases the rest.
  4. Clip — the “proximal” part. A single lucky batch is not allowed to rewrite the whole player. Here the clip is 0.1: the probability of an action may only move about 10% relative to the policy that collected the batch. That is why learning looks like a climb with dips, not a jump to a finished bot.
  5. Stay a little random. An entropy bonus (0.01) pays the agent to keep some variety, so it does not freeze on one trick. On stream, the window usually plays the greedy choice (highest probability). The eight trainers still sample, which is how they keep discovering.

Left: a huge update from one fluke. Right: PPO keeps the new player near the old one. Source of the idea: the PPO paper’s clipped objective.

Could it have been something other than PPO?

Yes. PPO is a popular default for “pixels in, buttons out,” not the only algorithm that can learn this game. The loop above (see, choose, play, score) stays the same. What changes is how the brain is updated after a batch of play. Different methods trade stability, sample efficiency, and engineering pain.

DQN / Rainbow

The original Atari neural-net player (2015) learned a score for each button — “how good is firing from this screen?” — then picked the best. It stores old games in a replay memory and learns from them again, so it often needs fewer new frames than PPO. Downside: it is a different kind of learner (values, not a policy), exploration is usually random fidgeting (ε-greedy), and the strong version (Rainbow) is a stack of extra tricks. Same eyes (Nature CNN); different training rule.

A2C / A3C

Older actor-critic cousins of PPO: play, estimate advantage, nudge the policy — but without the clip. A3C famously used many CPUs playing in parallel. Downside: one lucky batch can yank the player too far and collapse it. PPO exists largely to keep that idea and make the step size safe. For a single GPU and eight games, PPO is the calmer default.

TRPO

PPO’s immediate ancestor. It also says “do not jump too far,” but it enforces that with a heavier math constraint (a trust region) instead of a simple clip. Downside: more code, slower updates. PPO was written to get most of TRPO’s caution with first-year linear algebra.

SAC (what TrackMania uses here)

Soft Actor-Critic is excellent when the hands are a steering wheel: continuous actions, off-policy reuse of old driving. That is why the TrackMania notebooks on this site use SAC, not PPO. Downside for Invaders: six discrete buttons, not a stick. You can bend SAC to discrete actions, but it is the wrong shape. Using PPO here and SAC on the car is matching the algorithm to the controller.

Other real options, briefly: IMPALA (DeepMind) shines when you have a farm of actors, which we do not; vanilla policy gradient is the textbook version of “do more of what worked” and is too noisy to finish a 100 million step Atari run cleanly; model-based methods (MuZero and friends) imagine futures before acting and can be sample-efficient, at far more engineering and compute than this project.

Why PPO for this run. Space Invaders is discrete, we already had a CleanRL-style PPO trainer (same family as the 2D track), one GPU plus eight parallel games is PPO’s comfort zone, and the clip makes a long stream watchable: the agent drifts instead of randomly forgetting how to shoot. We did not pick it because the others cannot learn invaders — they can — we picked it because it is stable, standard, and already in this codebase.

Two different scores on stream

The overlay’s last finished number is the capture window — one game, greedy play, raw Atari points, updated only when that game ends. The training curve is the mean of the eight exploring games the learner actually updates on. Those games are noisier on purpose. If the two disagree, that is expected.

Atari 2600 scoring, briefly

Six rows of six invaders, 5–30 points by row, 630 for a full wave, 200 for the command ship. Waves do not end the game. You stop on lives, invaders landing, or the environment’s time cutoff. The 2600 scoreboard only shows four digits (9999) then rolls — that is a display limit.
